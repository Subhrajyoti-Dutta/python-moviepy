My Boss Mocked My Health. I Exposed His Pirated Software, Then Beat Him in Court.

INTRODUCTION

My boss mocked me for quitting after a health scare. So I reported the pirated software running on more than fifty school computers. I thought that was the end of it. Years later, he sued me over a school that didn't even exist anymore. That was his second expensive mistake.

SECTION 1: THE JOB I THOUGHT I WANTED

I'm a teacher with a degree in advertising, and I've worked with technology for more than twenty years. I found my love for teaching about a decade into that journey. Over time, I worked at schools large and small, taught classes, and helped build workbooks, video courses, and learning platforms. Eventually, I felt ready to manage a school rather than only teach at one.

A prestigious digital art school offered me the position of Teaching Manager. I would oversee teachers and the school's internal educational routines. It seemed like exactly the step forward I'd been hoping for. I expected long hours and difficult decisions. I did not expect to work for a man who treated intimidation like a normal management technique.

My boss had an impressive ability to demand things he'd never actually assigned. If a task wasn't finished, he would get angry, even when nobody knew he wanted it done. His temper was unpredictable. During one meeting, he picked up a large chair and pretended he was about to throw it across the room. He apparently thought this was hilarious.

Nobody laughed. Nobody even looked particularly surprised, because we all believed he might actually throw it. That was the atmosphere in the school. His idea of humor was reminding employees that he could hurt or humiliate them, and his idea of leadership was making sure everybody remained nervous enough to obey him.

SECTION 2: THE SWITCH FLIPS

In my country, a new employee could spend up to three months on a temporary arrangement with much less job security. My boss liked reminding people of that, often in the form of a joke that wasn't funny. He treated managers differently, though, and during my temporary period he praised my work constantly.

Then my temporary contract ended and I became a regular employee with the corresponding benefits and protections. Less than twenty-four hours later, the praise stopped. Suddenly I was getting screamed at, criticized, and blamed for problems that had never been my responsibility. It was such a sharp change that I could barely recognize the man who had hired me.

We paid an outside company to maintain the classroom computers every week. Nevertheless, every crash and software bug became my fault. One Saturday, after the students and staff had left, he ordered me to stay until all the computers were running smoothly. Apparently, being Teaching Manager also meant being personally responsible for every machine.

He also asked me to introduce him to a potential salesperson. I suggested a friend I'd known for years, making it clear that the hiring decision belonged to him. He hired my friend. Weeks later, my boss became angry because I'd gone for drinks with that same friend. He said managers should socialize with managers, not ordinary staff.

I reminded him that our friendship existed long before the job and told him he was welcome to join us for drinks. He didn't appreciate that response. Apparently, he wanted me to treat colleagues as people beneath me. I wasn't going to do that, and his hostility only intensified.

SECTION 3: THE HEALTH SCARE

Eventually, the stress started affecting my body. One day, I experienced chest pain and my left arm went completely numb. My friend called an ambulance. I went into my boss's office, where his fiancée was sitting, and told her I thought I might be having a heart attack and needed somewhere to lie down away from the students.

She was horrified. Fortunately, it wasn't a heart attack. It was an anxiety attack, and it wasn't the last one. I was thirty-six years old. My father had died of a heart attack more than twenty years earlier, and I saw my mother cry in a way I hadn't seen since his death. That experience changed my priorities.

I hadn't even been working there for six months. I decided my health and family mattered more than the management position, so I handed in thirty days' notice. I didn't blame my boss to his face. I explained that the stress was worrying me and promised to finish every project I'd started before leaving.

And I followed through. I updated courses, finished the new e-learning platform, arranged teachers for the next semester, and recorded promotional videos for the school's courses. I wanted to leave professionally, without abandoning the students or my coworkers. With less than a week remaining, I thought we'd managed to end things reasonably well.

Then he called me into his office and showed me a career progression plan. He pointed out how much I could earn in the future, provided I stopped being a sissy and did my job. That was his response to my health problems. The actual word he used was a homophobic slur, not merely the milder English term.

It wasn't accidental. Earlier, he'd complained about hiring women and gay men, then asked about my sexuality. I'd told him I was bisexual. He knew exactly how offensive his language was. I was finished trying to be gracious. I told him to deduct the remaining days from my final pay and walked away.

SECTION 4: A NEW BUSINESS AND AN OLD GRUDGE

Before I left, I'd told him I intended to open my own school. I hadn't told him that the salesperson I'd introduced was going to be my business partner. When my friend submitted his own thirty days' notice, the boss exploded and ordered him to leave immediately rather than work out the notice period.

Under the rules that applied, that meant paying him for the notice period along with other amounts owed, including commissions. Between those payments and my final settlement, we had enough money to launch our school. His temper had helped fund a business he clearly didn't want us to start.

Our new school focused mainly on comic-book classes. We didn't need a computer lab for that. When a particular class required a computer, students could bring their own. It was a different approach from the digital art school we'd left behind, and we were excited to build something for ourselves.

Before leaving the old school, we'd also been made to sign a strange confidentiality document. My boss had actually instructed me to draft it using internet searches and notes he emailed me. It contained numerous restrictive clauses and threatened a penalty of thirty thousand in local currency, about six thousand US dollars at the time.

I believed several clauses were illegal, and employees weren't even given copies. To me, the document seemed designed to frighten workers into silence. But a confidentiality agreement wasn't going to stop me from reporting what I believed was unlawful conduct. And there was something important I knew about the school's computers.

SECTION 5: THE SOFTWARE PIRACY REPORT

More than fifty classroom computers were running pirated software, according to what I'd seen. The list included Windows, Office, Adobe applications, Revit, Cinema 4D, 3ds Max, and other expensive programs. For a school teaching digital arts, those weren't minor tools. They were central to the operation, and proper licensing would have been costly.

I'd heard about a major retail chain in our state that faced severe financial consequences over software licensing. I knew piracy complaints were taken seriously at the time. So I submitted a report about the school's computers. I didn't have to confront my old boss directly. The reporting process would notify him.

There was a particularly funny feature of that system. The subject received the complaint almost immediately, with the reporter's identity omitted. About five minutes after our report, a complaint arrived accusing our new school of using thirty unlicensed copies of Windows and other programs. The accusation was remarkably similar to the one we'd just submitted.

There was one obvious problem: we didn't have thirty computers. We didn't have any classroom computers. My partner still spoke to the finance manager at our old school, so he sent him a picture of our classroom with no computer in sight. I can only imagine how that photograph went down with our former boss.

I didn't personally observe every step of the piracy investigation or receive a final breakdown of any penalties. But I did hear that my former boss's business was getting into serious trouble. And instead of concentrating on fixing his own problems, he continued trying to interfere with ours.

SECTION 6: HE COULDN'T LET GO

Within weeks, I heard that most of the remaining employees had left his school. Apparently, only an intern and some teachers without permanent contracts remained. Some of those teachers contacted us about running special classes. My old boss responded by threatening to terminate their contracts if they worked with our school.

Those teachers didn't have exclusivity clauses, and some called his bluff. According to what I heard, that resulted in more money being paid over contract breaches. He seemed determined to punish anyone who maintained a connection with us, even when doing so was hurting his own operation.

His school had once filled classes every six months and occupied nearly every available time slot. Later, I heard he could barely fill one shift. He began offering mainly evening classes and closing on some days because there weren't enough students or resources to keep operating normally.

Before I'd left, he'd spent a substantial sum on architectural plans to expand the school. I remembered hearing about forty thousand in local currency for that project alone. Now the rumors were about downsizing, closing the physical location, and switching to online-only courses, all before the pandemic.

I thought that was the end of my revenge story. I'd moved on and started a school, and my former boss seemed to be struggling with the consequences of his decisions. I wasn't planning another move against him. Years later, however, he decided he wasn't finished with me.

SECTION 7: A LAWSUIT AGAINST A CLOSED SCHOOL

My own school eventually closed. My friend and I discovered that being friends didn't make us compatible business partners. We sold what we could, settled the business, and went our separate ways. We weren't even friends anymore. It wasn't the outcome I'd wanted, but sometimes a business simply doesn't work out.

Months later, my former partner's sister, who was also his lawyer, told me our old boss was suing our school. The school that no longer existed. I tried searching the public records, but the proceedings were under a court-ordered confidentiality restriction. My lawyer advised me not to act until I was formally served.

So I waited. For years, nothing happened. Then, during lockdown, an official finally found me at home and served the papers. I had fifteen days to prepare a defense. For the first time, I could read exactly what my former boss claimed we'd done. My wife found me laughing in front of the computer.

He accused us of stealing his courses. His evidence included screenshots of our former website beside screenshots of his own, as though the existence of similar educational categories proved plagiarism. But he didn't provide actual course material demonstrating that we'd copied his lessons. The comparison was much weaker than he seemed to think.

For example, our website had a training category called Graphic Design, but we didn't offer a class with that exact title. Most of our teaching concerned comic books, which his school didn't teach. He even seemed offended that we'd entered the same general market, despite the fact that I had substantial educational experience before he ever hired me.

SECTION 8: THE MOST RIDICULOUS EVIDENCE

One passage in his filing was especially entertaining. Our website had explained that we drew on previous experience, learned from mistakes, and wanted to do things differently. My former boss presented that statement as a confession of plagiarism. Apparently, learning from experience was now evidence of stealing intellectual property.

I couldn't believe he'd spent years pursuing a case based on such weak comparisons. He called us cynical for competing with him, but competition wasn't proof of copying. He hadn't shown that our lessons contained his materials, and he couldn't make our comic-book classes identical to his courses just by putting screenshots side by side.

He also wanted the thirty-thousand penalty from that old employment document, plus damages. Remember, this was the document he'd made me draft, full of clauses I believed were invalid. He chose a legal process with potentially significant costs rather than bringing a smaller claim through a less expensive route.

I asked my lawyer if I could write the factual defense myself. I knew the history, the classes, and the conversations. I had saved emails, printed messages, and documents that could support what I said. She agreed to let me prepare it and handle the legal presentation.

I answered every allegation individually. I explained my previous teaching experience, identified the differences between our courses, and pointed out where his filing lacked evidence. I also made clear how strongly I objected to his attempt to punish me for starting a business after leaving his employment.

My lawyer, who was actually my mother-in-law, ended up using practically my entire statement. She normally didn't charge me because we were family. I was proud that I could help prepare the case, but I also knew the result would depend on what the judge made of the evidence.

SECTION 9: THE SECOND PAYOFF

The judge dismissed his case. My former boss didn't get the penalty or damages he demanded. Instead, he was ordered to pay the legal costs, including my lawyer's fees. He'd chosen to pursue a large claim and had ended up paying for a legal defeat.

That was particularly satisfying because my mother-in-law had represented me without charging me directly. The legal-fee award was a win for her as well. And unlike the earlier reports I'd heard about his financial struggles, this was an outcome I could point to directly: the judge rejected his case and ordered him to cover the costs.

I didn't need to invent a dramatic courtroom confrontation or imagine him begging for mercy. The facts were enough. He'd taken a school that had already closed to court over supposed plagiarism, offered weak evidence, and lost. He'd spent years keeping a grudge alive, only to make himself pay again.

The irony was that I hadn't gone looking for a second round of revenge. I thought we'd finished our business years earlier. He was the one who revived the dispute. He gave me access to his accusations, and I used the evidence I had to show why they didn't hold up.

SECTION 10: WHAT I TOOK FROM IT

My own story wasn't a perfect success story. My school closed, and my friendship with my business partner fell apart. Those things were disappointing. But they didn't prove that my former boss had been right to mistreat me, and they certainly didn't prove we'd stolen his courses.

I left his school because the stress was affecting my health. I had tried to give notice respectfully and finish every project before leaving. He responded with an insulting slur. Then he tried to intimidate former employees, made a ridiculous piracy accusation against a classroom without computers, and eventually sued over supposed similarities between different courses.

The first part of my revenge was reporting the pirated software I'd seen on more than fifty machines. The second part wasn't something I planned. It was defending myself when he brought the dispute back years later. In the end, the court ordered him to pay the costs of his own failed case.

I still don't claim to know every detail of his finances or exactly what happened behind the scenes after the software complaint. I know what I reported, what I later heard, and what the court decided in the lawsuit. That's enough for me. I don't need to exaggerate the story to enjoy the outcome.

He wanted to control the people who worked for him, then punish the people who left. But the more he tried to make my life difficult, the more expensive his own decisions became. I thought I'd finished with him years ago. He gave me a second chance at revenge, and he paid for that one too.
